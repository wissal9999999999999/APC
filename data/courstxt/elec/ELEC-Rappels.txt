Why review electricity in an electronics course?




            • Every electronic component — resistors, capacitors, transistors, op-amps — behaves
              according to electrical laws.
            • Knowing electricity helps explain why components behave the way they do in practical
              electronics (e.g., voltage drops, power ratings, signal losses).
            • Real-world debugging starts with basic electrical reasoning.
            • Necessary to evaluate the total power consumption of your embedded system.

The inductor and magnetic properties




       An inductor is a passive electronic component made from a coil of wire wrapped around a core,
       which can be air, iron or ferrite (a brittle material made from iron). If you pass current through an
       inductor, you create a magnetic field around the wire. If you change the current, increasing it or
       decreasing it, the magnetic flux around the coil changes, inducing a voltage across the inductor.
       That voltage, sometimes called back voltage, causes a current flow that opposes the main current.
       This property of inductors is known as self-inductance or simply inductance.

Inductors in circuits
            • DC voltage induces a flow of current in the inductor
            • magnetic field around the coils Increasing the current
            • increasing the strength of the magnetic field The changing magnetic field induces a back
              voltage This magnetic flux opposes any change in current This opposition is what is known as
              inductance Inductors act like an open circuit when DC is first applied (no current flows right
              away, and the entire source voltage drops across the inductor).
